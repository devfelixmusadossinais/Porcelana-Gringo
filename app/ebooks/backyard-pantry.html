WELCOME

The Kitchen Wisdom Grandma Knew

A little everyday comfort may be closer than you think: in your kitchen, your pantry, or a few pots by the window.

For generations, home cooks have grown herbs, gathered fresh ingredients, and shared simple recipes. A fragrant pot of tea, a well-seasoned soup, or fresh fruit at breakfast can bring pleasure and variety to an ordinary day.

Researchers have studied many of these ingredients. Some have well-established nutritional qualities; other uses rest mainly on tradition. This guide helps you understand the difference without turning a kitchen recipe into a health promise.

Welcome to The Backyard Pantry: Everyday Kitchen Wellness. Here you’ll find herbs, spices, fruits, everyday foods, and step-by-step recipes to help you feel more comfortable in the kitchen and build routines you enjoy.

If cooking with herbs and spices feels unfamiliar, you’re in the right place. Start with what you have, keep it simple, and let your tastes guide you.

Make room for yourself. Take an active role in your everyday wellness routine.

This is a guide to keep on the kitchen counter, with favorite pages marked and recipes you look forward to making again.

Start with one recipe that fits your needs and any dietary guidance you’ve received. If you enjoy it and it agrees with you, make it part of your week. Then try another when you’re ready.

Small, steady habits can make everyday life more enjoyable. Think of tending a garden: a little attention, a comfortable pace, and room to grow.

An Important Note

Please read

This guide offers general information about food, cooking, and everyday wellness. It is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease. It does not replace medical advice, prescribed medication, diagnosis, or care from a qualified healthcare professional.

Medications play an essential role in caring for many health conditions. Talk to your doctor before changing or stopping any medication.

Herbs and herbal products can interact with prescription and over-the-counter medicines. Before adding herbal teas, supplements, or concentrated preparations to your routine, talk with your doctor or pharmacist—especially if you have a chronic condition, take medication regularly, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are preparing something for a child. Recipes in this guide are intended for adults; ask a pediatric healthcare professional before offering herbal preparations to children.

Contact a healthcare professional for symptoms that persist, worsen, or concern you. Seek urgent medical help for trouble breathing, chest pain, or swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat. Do not delay medical care while trying a recipe.

The Golden Rule
Let your kitchen be part of your everyday care. Good food, enjoyable habits, and professional medical guidance belong together.

How to Use This Guide

Each ingredient and recipe carries an evidence label. These labels describe the research behind the food, its nutrients, or the ingredient being discussed. They do not promise a result from eating a food or making a recipe. Research on supplements or concentrated extracts does not necessarily apply to ordinary kitchen amounts.

Strong evidence

A substantial body of human research supports the specific nutritional role or ingredient finding described. This does not establish that a homemade recipe provides the same benefit.

Some evidence

Research exists, but studies may be small, inconsistent, or based on preparations different from those used in cooking.

Traditional use

A long history of culinary or household use, with limited scientific evidence for wellness benefits. Tradition alone does not establish safety or effectiveness.

Measurements used
1 cup= 8 fl oz
1 tbsp= 3 tsp
1 tsp= ⅓ tbsp
InfusionPour freshly boiled water over food-grade herbs, cover, and steep as directed.
DecoctionGently simmer roots or bark intended for food use, such as fresh ginger, in water for the time specified.
Kitchen tip

Choose packaged, food-grade dried herbs with clearly identified ingredients, the botanical name when available, and a best-by date. Avoid unidentified loose herbs. Essential oils and concentrated extracts are not substitutes for culinary herbs.

GingerStrong evidence
A Warming Kitchen Favorite

Ginger brings a bright, peppery flavor to sweet and savory dishes. It has a long culinary history, but most human research has tested supplements rather than ordinary food or tea. Those findings do not establish benefits from the kitchen preparations here. NIH NCCIH

How to use

For a mild ginger tea, gently simmer 2 thin slices of fresh ginger in 1 cup water for 5 minutes, then strain and let cool to a comfortable drinking temperature. You can also grate a little into soups, broths, and sauces. If it causes heartburn or irritation, skip it.

TurmericSome evidence
A Golden Touch for Everyday Meals

Turmeric adds earthy flavor and golden color to food. Curcumin, one of its naturally occurring compounds, has been widely studied, but findings remain uncertain and often involve concentrated products. They do not establish a wellness benefit from a sprinkle of turmeric in a meal. NIH NCCIH

How to use

Start with a small pinch in rice, soup, or roasted vegetables, and adjust to taste. Add black pepper or olive oil if you enjoy the flavor; neither is required. Keep turmeric as a seasoning rather than a daily dose. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before using turmeric or curcumin supplements, especially if you take medication, are pregnant, or are breastfeeding.

LemonSome evidence
Bright Flavor, Plus Vitamin C

Lemon adds freshness to meals and contributes vitamin C. Vitamin C helps the body absorb iron from plant foods; this established nutrient role does not mean lemon water offers a special wellness benefit. MedlinePlus

How to use

Add a squeeze to beans or leafy greens. For lemon water, stir 1–2 tsp juice into 1 cup water and adjust to taste. Keep the juice diluted when drinking it, and skip it if it causes heartburn or mouth irritation.

Evidence labels—Strong evidence / Some evidence / Traditional use—describe research on the food or herb. Studies may involve preparations different from those used in the kitchen. These labels do not establish benefits for the serving suggestions below or promise results.

HoneySome evidence
A little sweetness for a comforting cup

Honey adds gentle sweetness to a warm drink many people find soothing. Enjoy it as part of a quiet evening ritual.

How to use

Stir 1 tsp into comfortably warm tea.

Never give honey to a child younger than 12 months, including honey mixed into food or drinks. USDA honey safety guidance

GarlicSome evidence
Big flavor from a small clove

Garlic contains sulfur compounds, including allicin, which forms when a clove is crushed. In everyday cooking, its rich flavor brings vegetables, beans, and soups to life.

How to use

Crush a clove, let it rest for about 10 minutes, then cook it into a shared dish. Use an amount that tastes comfortable to you.

PeppermintSome evidence
A refreshing finish to a meal

Peppermint has a bright aroma and a long tradition as an after-meal tea. Most research involves peppermint oil preparations; findings do not establish the same benefits for leaf tea. NIH NCCIH peppermint guidance

How to use

Steep 5 to 8 fresh peppermint leaves in 1 cup hot water, covered, for 5 minutes. Strain and let cool to a comfortable drinking temperature.

ChamomileSome evidence
A floral cup for winding down

Chamomile tea is traditionally enjoyed during a quiet break or an evening wind-down routine. Research remains limited, so think of it as a pleasant ritual with a mild, floral flavor.

How to use

Steep 1 tbsp dried, food-grade chamomile flowers in 1 cup hot water, covered, for 10 minutes. Strain and let cool before sipping.

Avoid chamomile if you are allergic to ragweed or related flowers. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before use if you take medication, especially blood thinners or sedatives. Safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding is uncertain. NIH NCCIH chamomile guidance

CinnamonTraditional use
Warm flavor with a hint of sweetness

Cinnamon adds a sweet aroma without adding sugar. Try it when you want oatmeal, fruit, or a warm drink to taste a little more inviting.

How to use

Stir a pinch to ½ tsp into a recipe for oatmeal, cooked fruit, milk, or an unsweetened milk alternative. Adjust to taste.

OatsStrong evidence
A satisfying whole-grain staple

Oats contain beta-glucan, a soluble fiber that may support fullness as part of a balanced meal. They are an easy way to bring more whole grains into your routine.

How to use

Stir about 3 tbsp rolled oats into a smoothie or yogurt, or cook them into oatmeal according to package directions. Adjust the serving to your appetite.

FlaxseedSome evidence
Fiber and plant-based omega-3s

Flaxseed provides fiber and alpha-linolenic acid, a plant-based omega-3 fat. Add fiber gradually and drink water throughout the day, following any fluid limits your clinician has recommended.

How to use

A sweet way to add fiber

Prunes bring fiber, naturally occurring sorbitol, and a rich, fruity flavor to meals. They may support everyday digestive regularity as part of a varied diet.

How to use

Enjoy 3 to 5 prunes with breakfast or as a snack, with water. Start with fewer if dried fruit tends to cause gas or loose stools.

KiwiStrong evidence
Fresh fruit for your morning routine

Green kiwifruit has been studied in relation to digestive regularity. It also brings fiber, vitamin C, and a bright, tangy flavor to breakfast.

How to use

Enjoy 1 or 2 ripe kiwis on their own or sliced over oatmeal or yogurt. Choose a serving that suits your appetite; there is no need to eat them on an empty stomach.

Green TeaSome evidence
Antioxidants in your cup

Green tea contains catechins, plant compounds studied for their antioxidant properties. Research on concentrated extracts does not establish what an everyday cup of tea will do for you.

How to use

Steep 1 tsp of leaves in about 1 cup of water at 175°F, below boiling, for 3 minutes. Enjoy according to your caffeine tolerance; there is no daily target. Green tea contains caffeine and can interact with some medications. If you take medication or are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your doctor about drinking it regularly. See NIH NCCIH.

OnionSome evidence
An everyday source of quercetin

Onions contain quercetin, a plant compound studied for its antioxidant properties. Apples contain it, too. In the kitchen, onions bring flavor to everything from salads to soups.

How to use

Enjoy raw in salads or sautéed in soups and broths. Clean, well-rinsed outer skins can flavor stock; discard any moldy or spoiled layers and strain before serving.

AppleSome evidence
Fiber and plant compounds in every bite

Apples provide fiber, including pectin, along with plant compounds such as quercetin. Eating the peel adds fiber. Whole apples make a satisfying snack and may support everyday digestive regularity.

How to use

Wash well and enjoy with the peel, or steep clean apple peels with a little cinnamon and a whole clove. Strain before drinking. A peel infusion does not provide the fiber of a whole apple.

Throughout this guide, “Strong evidence,” “Some evidence,” and “Traditional use” describe the research or history behind the ingredient discussion. They do not promise results from a food, tea, or recipe.

Fennel SeedsSome evidence
A fragrant after-meal tradition

Fennel seeds have a long history in cooking and after-meal teas. Research on certain fennel preparations is limited and does not establish a benefit from a homemade cup. Many people enjoy their mild, licorice-like flavor.

How to use

Steep 1 tsp of food-grade fennel seeds in about 1 cup of hot water for 10 minutes, covered. Strain before sipping. Avoid concentrated preparations and essential oils. Ask your clinician before regular use if you take medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or plan to serve an herbal tea to a child.

Acerola CherrySome evidence
A bright source of vitamin C

Acerola cherries are rich in vitamin C, a nutrient involved in normal immune function and collagen production. They can add a pleasantly tart flavor to everyday foods.

How to use

Enjoy 5 to 10 fresh or frozen acerola cherries, removing any pits, or blend them into a smoothie. If acerola is hard to find, use strawberries or kiwi for another food source of vitamin C.

PropolisTraditional use
A bee product that calls for caution

Propolis is a resinous material bees make from plant substances. It has a history of traditional use, but research in people remains limited. It is not a routine pantry ingredient.

How to use

Skip propolis extracts in kitchen recipes. Propolis can cause allergic reactions, especially in people with allergies to bee products. If you are considering a propolis product, discuss it with your doctor or pharmacist first, particularly if you take medication. Avoid it during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and do not give it to children without pediatric guidance.

For a touch of sweetness in a warm drink, use a little honey if tolerated. Never give honey to a child younger than 12 months.

Ingredients That
Work Well Together

How simple pairings bring flavor, nutrients, and enjoyment to your meals.
The Backyard PantryEveryday Kitchen Wellness

Some ingredients taste better together. Certain pairings also help your body absorb nutrients or make a meal more satisfying. Here are seven easy combinations to try.

1

Turmeric + black pepper + a little fat

Use turmeric and a pinch of black pepper in a dish prepared with a little olive oil. They bring warmth, color, and flavor. Research on concentrated curcumin and piperine products does not establish the benefits of this kitchen combination. Some highly absorbable turmeric supplements have raised safety concerns. See NIH NCCIH.

2

Beans or dark leafy greens + lemon

Vitamin C helps your body absorb iron from plant foods. Add a squeeze of lemon to beans or greens, or serve them with bell peppers or tomatoes. See the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements.

3

Ginger + lemon + honey

A familiar combination for a warm, flavorful drink many people find soothing. Steep a few thin ginger slices in a cup of hot water, then add a small squeeze of lemon and a little honey to taste. Never give honey to a child younger than 12 months.

4

Oats + ground flaxseed + fruit

A practical breakfast with fiber, texture, and natural sweetness. Start with a little ground flaxseed and increase gradually as tolerated. Enjoy it with fluids as part of your usual routine.

5

Tomatoes + olive oil

A little olive oil adds flavor and helps your body absorb fat-soluble plant compounds such as lycopene. Try the pairing in a salad, a simple sauce, or roasted tomatoes.

6

Plain yogurt + banana or oats

Plain yogurt adds protein; banana or oats adds fiber and texture. Some yogurts also contain live cultures—check the label. Together, they make an easy breakfast or snack.

7

Chamomile + lemon balm

Two herbs many people enjoy as part of a quiet evening. If lemon balm is hard to find, plain chamomile makes a simple alternative. Use a food-grade tea prepared according to its package directions, without extracts or essential oils.

Chamomile can cause reactions in people allergic to ragweed or related plants and may interact with medications. Check with your clinician before regular herbal-tea use during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or before serving it to children. See NIH NCCIH.

Digestion and Everyday Regularity

Fiber-rich foods, enough fluids, and regular meals may support digestive well-being. Herbs can add flavor and make an everyday kitchen routine more enjoyable.

Evidence labels describe research on individual foods or ingredients. They do not establish that a recipe will produce a particular result. Research on supplements or concentrated extracts may not apply to ordinary food portions.

A quick safety note: This guide offers food and wellness ideas and does not replace medical advice. Herbs can interact with medications. If you take medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or plan to serve herbal drinks to a child, check with your doctor or pharmacist first. Use food-grade ingredients; do not substitute concentrated extracts or essential oils. Talk to your doctor before changing any medication. Seek medical care for persistent digestive changes, ongoing abdominal pain, vomiting, or blood in your stool. See NIH NCCIH and MedlinePlus.

01Ginger and Mint TeaSome evidence
For: A warm, fragrant pause after a meal.
Research on ginger includes preparations different from this kitchen tea; findings do not establish a benefit from this blend.
Ingredients
  • 1 cup water
  • 2 thin slices fresh ginger
  • 5 fresh mint leaves
Directions
  1. 1.Bring the water and ginger to a boil, then simmer for 5 minutes.
  2. 2.Remove from the heat, add the mint, and cover.
  3. 3.Steep for 5 minutes, strain, and let cool to a comfortable sipping temperature.
Tip: Try it unsweetened, or add a small drizzle of honey once it is warm. Never give honey to a child under 12 months.

07Golden MilkSome evidence
For: A cozy kitchen ritual with turmeric and ginger.
Research on turmeric is inconclusive and often involves concentrated products; it does not establish a wellness benefit from this drink.
Ingredients
  • 1 cup milk or unsweetened plant-based milk
  • ½ tsp ground turmeric
  • A pinch of black pepper, optional
  • 1 thin slice fresh ginger
  • 1 tsp olive oil or coconut oil, optional
  • Honey to taste, optional
Directions
  1. 1.Gently heat the milk with the turmeric, ginger, and optional pepper, without bringing it to a rolling boil.
  2. 2.Keep over low heat for 5 minutes, stirring occasionally.
  3. 3.Strain, stir in the optional oil, and sweeten with a little honey if you like. Let cool to a comfortable sipping temperature.
Tip: Pepper adds warmth, and oil adds richness; both are optional. Enjoy this drink for its flavor and comforting warmth. Concentrated turmeric products differ from kitchen spices and can have medication interactions and other safety concerns. See NIH NCCIH.

09Golden Vegetable SoupSome evidence
For: A simple, warming bowl with colorful vegetables and pantry spices.
The label refers to research on individual ingredients, not a demonstrated benefit from this soup.
Ingredients
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • ½ onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 carrot, diced
  • 1 zucchini, diced
  • 1 tsp ground turmeric
  • 1 pinch of black pepper
  • 1 tsp grated fresh ginger
  • 3 cups water
  • Salt and chopped parsley, to taste
Directions
  1. 1.Heat the olive oil in a saucepan. Add the onion, garlic, and ginger, and cook until the onion softens.
  2. 2.Add the turmeric, pepper, carrot, and zucchini. Stir for 1 minute.
  3. 3.Add the water and simmer for about 20 minutes, until the vegetables are tender.
  4. 4.Finish with parsley and season to taste.
Tip: Makes 3 servings. Refrigerate promptly in shallow containers, or freeze in individual portions.

Sleep and Relaxation

A good night’s rest starts with everyday habits. A cup of tea can be a pleasant cue to slow down and settle into your evening routine. If sleep difficulties persist or affect your daily life, talk with your doctor.

11Evening Chamomile and Lemon Balm TeaSome evidence
For: A warm, fragrant cup to enjoy while winding down.
Research on these herbs is limited; it does not establish that this homemade tea improves sleep.
Ingredients
  • 1 cup boiling water
  • 1 tsp dried, food-grade chamomile flowers
  • ½ tsp dried, food-grade lemon balm leaves
Directions
  1. 1.Place the herbs in a cup and pour the boiling water over them.
  2. 2.Cover and steep for 5 minutes.

Safety and Everyday Care

Natural does not mean risk-free.

This guide offers ideas for cooking and everyday well-being. It does not replace medical advice or individualized nutrition guidance.

Foods, herbs, and supplements can interact with medications. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before making herbs a regular part of your routine, especially if you take prescription or over-the-counter medicines. Talk to your doctor before changing any medication.

If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, check with your healthcare professional before using herbal teas or supplements. Ask a pediatric healthcare professional before offering herbal preparations to children; adult recipes and portions are not automatically appropriate for them.

Use herbs in ordinary cooking amounts. Avoid concentrated herbal preparations, essential oils taken by mouth, and homemade extracts. Choose ingredients you can identify confidently, and account for food allergies and intolerances.

Seek medical advice for symptoms that persist, worsen, or interfere with daily life. Get urgent medical help for trouble breathing, fainting, severe abdominal pain, or signs of a serious allergic reaction.

Where to read more

For general information about herbs, food, and nutrition, consult NIH NCCIH, MedlinePlus, and the Dietary Guidelines for Americans.

Know the signs
When to seek medical care

Call 911 for trouble breathing, swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, fainting, or other signs of a medical emergency. Seek prompt medical care for a high or persistent fever, severe pain, blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, severe dizziness, or new or unexplained swelling. Contact your doctor about symptoms that worsen, keep returning, or last more than a few days. Do not wait if symptoms are severe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to common questions.
The Backyard PantryEveryday Kitchen Wellness

What should I know about medication side effects?

Medications have benefits and possible side effects. If something is bothering you, talk with your doctor or pharmacist. Your doctor can review your options and adjust your prescription when appropriate. Talk to your doctor before changing any medication, and do not stop or reduce it on your own.

Can I replace my medication with these recipes?

No. These recipes are for everyday meals, drinks, and kitchen routines. They do not replace prescribed medication or medical care. Keep following your care plan, and check with your doctor or pharmacist about food and herb interactions.

How soon might I notice a difference?

There is no guaranteed result or timeline. Focus on meals you enjoy, a varied diet, and habits you can comfortably maintain. Foods such as kiwi and prunes can fit into that routine, but persistent changes in digestion or other symptoms deserve medical attention.

Can I use these recipes during pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

Many herbs have not been adequately studied during these stages. Familiar foods in ordinary serving sizes can often fit into your usual diet, depending on your individual needs. Check with your prenatal care provider before using herbal teas, supplements, or concentrated preparations.

What about children?

Never give honey to a child younger than 12 months. Ask your child’s pediatrician before offering herbal teas or concentrated preparations. Do not assume an adult recipe is suitable for a child.

Are lemongrass and lemon balm the same plant?

No. Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) is a grass. Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) is a leafy herb in the mint family. In Brazil, the name erva-cidreira may refer to lemon balm or other plants, depending on the region. Look for the botanical name on the package so you know what you are buying.

Can I make a batch and save it?

Herbal tea: brew a fresh cup when you want one, following the package directions. Diluted citrus drinks and juices: make small batches, refrigerate promptly, and use within 24 hours; choose pasteurized juice. Honey-lemon mixture: refrigerate in a clean, covered container and use within 3 days. Broth: refrigerate in shallow containers and use within 3 days, or freeze.

Keep your refrigerator at 40°F or below. Refrigerate perishable preparations within 2 hours, or within 1 hour if the temperature is above 90°F. These storage limits assume clean preparation and proper refrigeration. See FDA food storage guidance.
